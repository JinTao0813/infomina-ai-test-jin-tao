The claim ladder

1ObservedLarge within-city variation; positive weekend deltas for most eligible users; venue and category diversity differ; metrics depend on observed history.
2InterpretedCity stereotypes are less useful than individual context; novelty may need multiple dimensions and uncertainty.
3HypothesizedTransparent novelty adjustment might make venue suggestions more useful.
4ImplementedA deterministic ranker over synthetic profiles and privacy-safe historical candidates makes that behavior inspectable.
5Not establishedWhether users want it, whether rankings are relevant, and whether outcomes improve without harm.

Why Context-Aware Discovery was selected

It connects all principal findings while avoiding a city stereotype. Profile venue/category entropy provides bounded history signals; confidence limits those signals; a reliable weekend difference can add a modest context adjustment; and explicit user preference remains strongest. Competing ideas were narrower or less defensible: weekend-only mode ignores other evidence, merchant insights risk confusing check-ins with demand, and entropy alone is not a privacy-risk score.

What the prototype actually does

  • Offers four synthetic histories and a weekday/weekend context.
  • Maps explicit familiar/balanced/something-new choices to 0.20/0.50/0.80.
  • Lets explicit intent contribute 70% of applied discovery; inferred profile diversity contributes 30%.
  • For confidence below 0.50, forces the inferred portion to neutral and suppresses the weekend adjustment.
  • Reranks the same sixteen pseudonymous aggregate candidates with transparent components.
  • Uses inverse aggregate historical popularity as candidate novelty and profile category membership as a separate familiarity signal.
Two different “novelty” ideas: profile entropy summarizes diversity in a synthetic history. Candidate novelty is inverse popularity in the historical sample. Category familiarity says whether the candidate category occurs in that profile. No venue receives an entropy score.

The future experiment

  • Unit: user, preventing one person from experiencing conflicting policies.
  • Control: baseline relevance ranking.
  • Treatment: context-aware ranking over the same pool with the same explicit override.
  • Primary: save or selection rate per recommendation impression.
  • Secondary: category discovery, catalog coverage, and return engagement.
  • Guardrails: hides/dismissals, early exits, repetition, travel distance, latency, sparse-history performance, and differential harm.
  • Segments: baseline entropy, confidence, context, city, and explicit choice.

Duration must cover complete weekday/weekend cycles. Before running, define the minimum detectable effect, power, stopping rule, and privacy/retention plan. Analyze intention-to-treat first; subgroup analyses should be pre-specified rather than mined.

Success and abandonment

Proceed only if save/selection improves meaningfully without guardrail harm and the effect is not confined to one convenient segment. Redesign or abandon if gains are trivial, harms rise, current candidate quality is poor, sparse users suffer, or the feature requires unjustified location-history retention.

“The historical analysis motivated a mechanism, and the prototype makes that mechanism inspectable. It does not validate it. I would compare baseline and context-aware ranking at user level over the same pool, measure save or selection per impression, and stop if user-experience, distance, latency, sparse-history, fairness, privacy, or trust guardrails worsen.”

Retrieve

What did building the prototype add to the evidence?
It added implementation evidence: the hypothesis can be expressed as transparent deterministic behavior with safety boundaries. It added no evidence that the behavior improves recommendations.
Why randomize by user?
A personalization policy acts across a person's impressions; user-level assignment avoids cross-arm contamination and inconsistent experiences.
